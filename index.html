How a round works

  1. Everyone rolls their dice and keeps them hidden.
  2. The first player makes a bid: a claim like “at least four 5s” across all the dice on the table.
  3. Going around the table, each player either raises (more dice, or the same number of dice with a higher face) or calls “liar!” on the last bid.
  4. On a call, everyone reveals. If there really are that many, the caller loses a die; if not, the bidder does.
  5. With 1s wild, 1s count as every face (and you can't bid on 1s).
  6. The loser starts the next round. Lose all your dice and you're out. Last player standing wins.

Assignment: design a Liar's Dice bot

Your goal is to invent a strategy for Liar's Dice and write it down clearly enough that it can be turned into a bot. Your bot will then play thousands of games against the computer strategies and your classmates' bots. Your strategy stays secret: classmates will see your bot's results, but not how it works.

  1. Learn the app (5 minutes)
  2. Try five experiments to see what works (nothing to hand in)
  3. Design your strategy using the checklist
  4. Fill in the form at the bottom and send it to your teacher

Your bot will then compete in the class tournament, described below.

If you haven't played Liar's Dice before, read How a round works first.

The class tournament: what you're preparing for

Every bot in the class will compete in a two-stage tournament, played with the standard rules (5 dice each, 1s wild).

Stage 1

Qualifying rounds

Your bot plays a long series of games. Each game is at a table of 6 bots chosen at random from the class, so you'll face many different combinations of opponents. Bots are ranked by how often they win.

→
Stage 2

The final

The 6 best bots from qualifying play each other at one table, hundreds of times. The bot that wins the most games is the class champion.

What this means for your strategy

  • Most of your games start with 6 players. Test your ideas at a 6-player table, not just one-on-one.
  • Every game ends one-on-one. If your bot survives, it will play the endgame against a single opponent with only a few dice left, so think about whether your strategy should change then.
  • Winning is what counts. Surviving longest without winning doesn't earn points, so playing it safe forever won't be enough.
  • You'll meet the same opponents many times. A bot that learns how each opponent plays from past rounds can take advantage of it.
  • Luck evens out. With this many games, a strategy that's even a little better will usually rise to the top.

Part 2 · Five experiments

You don't need to hand anything in for this part. These experiments are here to help you discover what works before you design your own strategy, so take notes if that helps you.

  1. Read the table. Go to Watch a game, untick Show everyone's dice, and step through a few rounds. Before each call, guess whether the bid is true. How often were you right? What made a bid look suspicious?
  2. Find the best threshold. Set up just two players: Probabilistic vs Own Dice Only. Run 500 games. Then move Probabilistic's slider (when it calls liar) to 0.2, 0.4 and 0.6, running 500 games each time. Which setting wins most, and why might calling too often or too rarely both be bad?
  3. Does table size matter? Run Bluffer against one Probabilistic player, then against five Probabilistic players. Compare Bluffer's win rate with the dashed line each time. Does bluffing work better at a big table or a small one?
  4. Luck vs skill. Pick any two strategies. Run 100 games three times with different seeds, then 2000 games. How much do the 100-game results jump around compared with 2000? How many games would you want before deciding one strategy is better?
  5. Scout a classmate. Add one or two 🔒 student bots and watch a few of their games. What do you think they do? What do they seem to be good or bad at?

Part 3 · Design your strategy

What your bot can know

  • Its own dice.
  • How many dice each player has, and how many are on the table in total.
  • Every bid made so far this round, in order, and who made it.
  • Every past round in this game, in full: everyone's dice (they're revealed at each call), every bid, who called and who lost.
  • Anything it chooses to remember across rounds and games (for example, "Pat bluffs a lot").
  • It can use chance, for example "bluff 1 time in 4".

What it can't know

  • Anyone else's dice in the current round.
  • Which strategy each opponent is using (but it can learn from how they play).

What it can do on its turn

  • Raise: more dice (any value), or the same number of dice with a higher value.
  • Call liar on the last bid (not on the opening bid of a round).

Checklist: a complete strategy answers all of these

Your bot will face every situation, so your strategy must say exactly what to do in each one. If it doesn't, someone else has to guess what you meant.

  1. Opening bid. What do you bid when you go first in a round?
  2. When to call liar. A precise rule with numbers. Not "if it seems too high", but "if the bid is more than 1 above the number I expect", or "if the chance it's true is below 30%".
  3. How to raise. Which value and how many dice? Do you stay on the same value or switch?
  4. Ties. If two values are equally good (for example, you hold two 3s and two 5s), which one do you pick?
  5. When your preferred raise isn't allowed. For example, the bid is already higher than you'd like to go. What then?
  6. Does anything change with the number of players left, or the number of dice left (for example, when it's just you and one other player)?
  7. Optional: Do you use other players' bids? If someone bids 5s, do you assume they have some 5s?
  8. Optional: Do you learn from past rounds? What do you track, and how does it change your play?
  9. Optional: Do you bluff? When, how often, and with what bid?

Tips for writing it clearly

  • Say "at least". A bid of "four 5s" means at least four. If you use a probability, say whether you mean exactly or at least that many.
  • Use "bid" and "call liar" for different things. "Call" on its own can be confusing.
  • Remember wild 1s. With 1s wild, a 1 counts as every value. Say whether your counts include them (they usually should).
  • Give numbers, not feelings. "Bluff about 1 time in 5", not "bluff sometimes".
  • Expected count is a useful tool. Each die you can't see shows a given value 1 time in 3 when 1s are wild (1 in 6 when they aren't). So with 12 hidden dice, expect about 4 of any value, plus whatever you hold.
  • Read it back as a robot would. Pick a few situations and follow your rules word by word. Do they always tell you exactly one thing to do?

Two complete examples

These are made up for illustration. Yours can be simpler or more complex, as long as it's complete.

Example 1: "The Counter" (simple, uses only its own dice)
  1. Opening bid: I bid the number of dice I hold of my most common value (counting wild 1s). Ties: the higher value. Example: holding 2, 3, 3, 1, 6, I hold three 3s (two 3s plus the wild 1), so I bid three 3s.
  2. Expected count: for any value, my matching dice plus (number of dice I can't see ÷ 3).
  3. Call liar if the bid's quantity is more than 1 above the expected count for its value.
  4. Raise: if I hold at least two dice matching the current bid's value, I bid one more of that value. Otherwise I switch to my most common value (ties: higher value) at the smallest amount the rules allow.
  5. If that raise would need more dice than are on the table, I call liar instead.
  6. Number of players / dice left: no change.
  7. Other players' bids, history, bluffing: not used.
Example 2: "The Detective" (reads bids, learns, bluffs)
  1. Memory: after every round (when dice are revealed), for each bid an opponent made, I note whether they held at least two dice of that value (counting 1s). For each opponent I keep their "honest score": the share of their bids where that was true. Everyone starts at 50%.
  2. Reading bids: when judging a bid on some value, I count each opponent with an honest score of 60% or more who has bid that value this round as holding two of it.
  3. Chance a bid is true: the chance that at least the number of dice still needed show up among the dice I can't see and haven't counted, where each shows the value 1 time in 3.
  4. Call liar if that chance is below 30%. With only two players left, below 40%.
  5. Opening bid: one of my most common value (ties: the higher value).
  6. Raise: the smallest legal bid on whichever value has the highest chance of being true. Ties: fewer dice, then the lower value.
  7. Bluff: when three or fewer players remain, 1 time in 5 (decided at random) I instead bid the smallest legal amount of a value I hold none of, as long as that bid would be at least 50% likely with all dice unknown. If no value qualifies, I raise normally.
  8. If no raise is possible, I call liar.
